Thalassemia Carrier Screening in Gujarat — What Couples Need to Know

By Lakshita Chauhan Thakore, CGC — BGCI Certified Genetic Counselor, Ahmedabad  |  August 2026

Gujarat has one of the highest rates of beta-thalassemia carriers in India. For couples planning a pregnancy, this is not a reason to panic — it is a reason to test. A simple blood test before pregnancy can tell you whether you are a carrier, and if both partners are carriers, what your options are. This guide explains everything you need to know.

Thalassemia is particularly prevalent in communities including Lohanas, Patels, Kutchi Bhatias, Sindhi communities, and coastal Gujarati populations. However, all couples in Gujarat planning a pregnancy should consider carrier screening — not just those from traditionally higher-risk communities.

What Is Beta-Thalassemia?

Beta-thalassemia is an inherited blood disorder that affects the production of haemoglobin — the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. It is caused by mutations (changes) in the HBB gene.

Beta-Thalassemia Minor (Carrier / Trait)

People with one changed copy of the HBB gene are called carriers or have "thalassemia minor" / "thalassemia trait." They are generally healthy with no serious symptoms, though they may have mild anaemia. They do not require treatment. However, if two carriers have a child together, that child has a 25% chance of inheriting thalassemia major.

Beta-Thalassemia Major (Cooley's Anaemia)

Children with two changed copies of the HBB gene (one from each parent) have thalassemia major — a severe condition that typically becomes apparent within the first year of life. Without treatment, it is life-threatening. With treatment, children require regular blood transfusions every 2–4 weeks for life, iron chelation therapy, and specialist haematology follow-up. A bone marrow transplant can be curative but is complex and not always available or successful.

⚠️
Important distinction: Being a thalassemia carrier (thalassemia minor) does NOT mean you have thalassemia disease. Most carriers are completely healthy. The risk arises only when both partners are carriers — which is why testing both partners is essential.

If Both Partners Are Carriers — Understanding the Risks

When both partners carry one changed copy of the HBB gene, each pregnancy has the following probability:

50%
Carrier (thalassemia minor) — healthy, no treatment needed
25%
Not a carrier — completely unaffected
25%
Thalassemia major — requires lifelong treatment
75%
Unaffected by thalassemia major (carrier + non-carrier)

These probabilities apply independently to each pregnancy — they are not cumulative. A couple who has already had an affected child still has a 25% chance with every subsequent pregnancy.

The Thalassemia Carrier Test — What It Involves

Carrier screening for thalassemia requires a simple blood test. There is no special preparation required — no fasting, no hospital admission.

Test What It Detects When Used
CBC (Complete Blood Count) Low MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume, <80 fL) and MCH (<27 pg) suggest possible carrier status First-line screening — available everywhere
Haemoglobin Electrophoresis / HPLC Elevated HbA2 (>3.5%) confirms beta-thalassemia carrier; detects HbS (sickle cell) and other variants Confirmatory test after low MCV/MCH
HBB Gene Sequencing Identifies the specific mutation — critical for PGT planning and precise prenatal diagnosis When HPLC is positive; before IVF/PGT
Alpha Globin Gene Analysis Detects alpha-thalassemia — a related condition; important in certain ethnic groups When CBC is abnormal but HPLC is normal

For couples planning pregnancy, both partners should be tested. If only one partner is a carrier, no further action beyond awareness is needed. Testing the second partner is only clinically necessary if the first is positive — but testing both simultaneously saves time.

If You're Both Carriers — Your Reproductive Options

Discovering that both partners are beta-thalassemia carriers does not mean you cannot have children. It means you have decisions to make — and time to make them thoughtfully. A genetic counselor will discuss all options without bias:

Option 1: Natural Conception with Prenatal Diagnosis

Conceive naturally and test the fetus during pregnancy. Prenatal testing options include:

  • Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS) — at 10–13 weeks, a small sample of placental tissue is taken and tested for the HBB mutations. Results in 1–2 weeks.
  • Amniocentesis — at 15–20 weeks, amniotic fluid is sampled. Slightly lower miscarriage risk than CVS.

If the fetus is found to have thalassemia major, couples can choose to continue the pregnancy (with full information about the child's medical needs) or, within the legal framework of India's MTP Act, consider termination.

Option 2: IVF with Preimplantation Genetic Testing (PGT-M)

Embryos created through IVF are tested on Day 5 for the specific HBB mutations. Only unaffected embryos (thalassemia-free) are transferred to the uterus. This means the pregnancy itself is free of thalassemia major — no invasive prenatal testing is needed.

PGT-M for thalassemia requires a preparation phase of 3–4 months before egg retrieval, during which a custom genetic probe is designed based on both parents' specific mutations. This is why preconception genetic counseling is essential — it gives time to prepare.

Option 3: Donor Gametes

Using donor eggs or donor sperm from a tested, non-carrier donor eliminates the 25% risk. An option for couples who prefer to avoid prenatal or preimplantation testing.

Option 4: Adoption

Some couples choose not to have biological children and explore adoption instead.

💬
No pressure, no judgment: A genetic counselor's role is not to recommend a specific option. It is to ensure you understand all options fully — their medical implications, practical feasibility in India, and emotional considerations — so you can make the decision that is right for your family.

Gujarat's Thalassemia Prevention Programme

Gujarat is one of the few Indian states with a structured thalassemia prevention initiative. The Gujarat Thalassemia Prevention Programme (GTPP) has mandated premarital carrier screening in educational institutions and some districts. This screening uses basic CBC and HPLC testing.

However, the programme has limitations — it primarily screens for thalassemia and sickle cell disease, and coverage is uneven. Many couples, particularly those married outside the programme or who missed college-based screening, remain unscreened. Additionally, the programme's mutation-level testing (HBB gene sequencing) is not universally offered — which means couples who test positive at the HPLC level may not have the specific mutation identified, making PGT planning impossible without further testing.

Frequently Asked Questions

I've been told I have "thalassemia minor." Does this mean I have thalassemia?

No. Thalassemia minor (thalassemia trait) means you carry one changed copy of the HBB gene. You are a carrier. You are generally healthy and do not have thalassemia disease. The concern arises only if your partner is also a carrier — in which case each pregnancy has a 25% risk of thalassemia major. Testing your partner is the most important next step.

My HPLC shows elevated HbA2. What does that mean?

Elevated HbA2 (greater than 3.5%) is the hallmark finding of beta-thalassemia carrier status. It confirms that you carry one copy of a beta-globin gene mutation. You should now have your partner tested, and ideally have your specific mutation identified (HBB gene sequencing) so that your carrier status is fully characterised for future family planning.

My CBC showed low MCV. Do I have thalassemia?

Low MCV (Mean Corpuscular Volume, below 80 fL) is the first indicator of possible thalassemia carrier status, but it is not diagnostic on its own. Iron deficiency anaemia also causes low MCV. The next step is HPLC testing after ensuring iron stores are normal. A genetic counselor can guide you through the correct sequence of tests.

We already have one child with thalassemia major. What are our options for the next pregnancy?

You have the same 25% risk with each subsequent pregnancy. Your best options are PGT-M (IVF with genetic testing of embryos) or prenatal diagnosis in the next pregnancy (CVS or amniocentesis). Since you already have an affected child, your specific HBB mutations are likely known — which means PGT-M probe preparation can start immediately.
