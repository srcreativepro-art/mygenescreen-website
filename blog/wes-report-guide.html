What Is a WES Report? A Patient's Complete Guide

By Lakshita Chauhan Thakore, CGC — BGCI Certified Genetic Counselor, Ahmedabad  |  August 2026

You've just received a Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) report — several pages of dense genetic terminology, variant classifications, and clinical findings. For most patients and families, this document feels overwhelming. This guide explains exactly what it means, what each classification signals, and what you should do next.

💡
Quick Answer: A WES report tells you about changes (variants) found in the protein-coding regions of your DNA. Each variant is classified from Pathogenic (disease-causing) to Benign (harmless). The report alone doesn't make medical decisions — a genetic counselor interprets it in the context of your personal and family history.

What Is Whole Exome Sequencing?

Your genome contains about 3 billion base pairs of DNA, but only about 2% of it codes for proteins. This coding region is called the exome. Whole Exome Sequencing reads all ~20,000 protein-coding genes to look for changes (variants) that might cause disease.

WES is typically ordered when:

  • A child has unexplained developmental delay, intellectual disability, or multiple birth defects
  • Routine genetic tests (chromosomal microarray, gene panels) have not provided a diagnosis
  • A rare or undiagnosed disease is suspected
  • A family has a known genetic condition and needs detailed analysis

WES finds a diagnosis in approximately 30–50% of rare disease cases where previous testing was negative — making it one of the most powerful diagnostic tools in genetics today.

Understanding the 5 Variant Classifications

Every variant found in your WES report is classified into one of five categories as per international ACMG/AMP guidelines. Here is what each means:

Classification What It Means Action Needed
Pathogenic Definitively disease-causing. Strong scientific evidence links this variant to disease. Medical management, family testing, specialist referral
Likely Pathogenic Almost certainly disease-causing (≥90% probability). Treated similarly to Pathogenic in clinical practice. Medical management, genetic counseling for family
VUS (Variant of Uncertain Significance) A change exists, but there isn't enough evidence yet to classify it as harmful or harmless. This is the most common and most misunderstood category. Watch-and-wait; re-evaluate in 12–18 months as evidence accumulates
Likely Benign Probably harmless. Not expected to cause disease. No clinical action required
Benign Definitively harmless. A normal variant found in the general population. No action required
⚠️
About VUS: A "Variant of Uncertain Significance" does NOT mean you have a disease. It means the scientific community does not yet have enough data to classify it either way. Roughly 50% of WES reports contain at least one VUS. Many VUS findings are re-classified to Benign as databases grow — which is why a periodic re-review of your report is valuable.

Key Sections of a WES Report

1. Primary Findings

The main section — variants in genes directly related to your clinical indication (the reason WES was ordered). This is where Pathogenic or Likely Pathogenic variants will appear if a diagnosis was reached.

2. Secondary / Incidental Findings

Variants found in genes not related to your primary concern, but medically significant — for example, a BRCA1 variant found in a child tested for a developmental condition. You have the right to opt out of receiving secondary findings before testing. Guidelines recommend secondary findings be disclosed for 73 specific high-impact genes (ACMG SF v3.2).

3. Carrier Status Findings

Variants in recessive genes where carrying one copy typically doesn't cause disease in the individual but is relevant for family planning. Common examples: CFTR (Cystic Fibrosis), SMN1 (SMA), HBB (Thalassemia).

4. Negative Result

A "negative" WES does not mean no genetic cause exists. It means no disease-causing variant was identified in the exome at this time. Intronic variants, structural changes, and gene expression issues are not captured by WES — Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) may be the next step.

What Happens After You Get a WES Report?

  1. Book a genetic counseling session — This is the essential step. A genetic counselor reads the report in the context of your family history, clinical presentation, and medical records. Raw variant data without clinical context can be misleading.
  2. Specialist follow-up — If a Pathogenic variant is identified, you'll be referred to the relevant specialist (neurologist, cardiologist, metabolic specialist, etc.).
  3. Family testing — First-degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) may be offered targeted testing for the identified variant. This is called "cascade testing."
  4. Re-analysis — If the report is negative or contains only VUS findings, schedule a re-analysis in 12–18 months. Genetic databases grow every year, and a variant that was uncertain today may be classified as Pathogenic or Benign by then.

WES vs WGS — What's the Difference?

WES sequences the coding regions (~2% of the genome) — faster, less expensive, finds most known disease-causing variants.

WGS (Whole Genome Sequencing) sequences the entire genome — more comprehensive, detects structural variants, copy number changes, and intronic variants that WES misses. Recommended when WES is inconclusive and a genetic cause is still strongly suspected.

My report says "Negative" — does that mean I don't have a genetic condition?

Not necessarily. A negative WES covers the exome but misses ~98% of the genome. If clinical suspicion remains high, WGS is the next step. A genetic counselor can help determine whether further testing is warranted based on your clinical picture.
